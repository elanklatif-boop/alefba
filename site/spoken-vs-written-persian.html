Why nân becomes nun: spoken and written Persian

Books say nân, khâne and bârân. Families say nun, khune and bârun. Here's what's going on, which words change, and which form to learn.

In everyday Persian, a long â followed by n or m is usually said u. Books write nân (نان, bread), but people say nun (نون). Khâne (house) becomes khune, and bârân (rain) becomes bârun. The spelling doesn't change: only the sound does.

Why do Iranians say nun instead of nân?

In everyday Persian, especially the Persian of Tehran, a long â followed by n or m is usually said as u. So nân (bread) is said nun, and khâne (house) is said khune. The spelling stays the same: نان is still written نان.

It's one of the first things that surprises people who learned Persian from a textbook, and one of the first things heritage learners notice: the word they hear at home isn't the word in the book. Both are right. They're the written form and the spoken form of the same word.

In text messages and chats, people often spell the spoken form the way it sounds, like نون and خونه. In books, newspapers and anything formal, it's always the written form.

Does every â before n or m become u?

No. It happens in common everyday words, but not in all of them. سلام salâm (hello) never becomes “salum”, and نام nâm (name) stays nâm. Formal words, religious words and words you mostly meet in writing usually keep their â.

There's no rule that tells you which words change, so the best way to learn them is to hear them. A good rule of thumb: the more everyday the word, the more likely it is to change.

Should I learn the written or the spoken form?

Both. Learn to read the written form, because that's how words are spelled in books, on signs and in the news. Say the spoken form when you talk. Saying nân isn't wrong, but in everyday conversation it sounds like reading aloud from a book, a bit like saying “I do not know” instead of “I don't know”.

You'll also hear the written forms out loud: in poetry, on the news, in speeches and in songs.

Is this only in Tehran?

The u sound is typical of Tehran and of the everyday Persian you hear in Iranian films, series and most Iranian families abroad. Other cities have their own accents. Dari, the Persian of Afghanistan, keeps the â, so nân stays nân, and in Tajik it becomes non.

Other ways spoken Persian is different

The â-to-u change is only one difference. Verbs get shorter too: mikhâham (I want) is said mikhâm, and khub ast (it's good) is said khube. See how Persian verbs get shorter when people talk.

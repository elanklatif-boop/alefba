How to teach your child Persian at home

Practical ideas for families raising children with Persian outside Iran, from the first words to reading the alphabet.

Use Persian every day, even a little: at meals, at bedtime, on calls with grandparents. Keep practice short and regular, give your child real reasons to use it, and start reading with the alphabet a few letters at a time. Games, stories and songs help it feel like fun, not homework.

Where should I start?

Start with the Persian your child already hears: greetings, family words such as mâmân and bâbâ, food, and everyday phrases such as befarmâ and dastet dard nakone. Words they can use the same day are the easiest to keep.

A first handful, recorded by a Persian speaker from the family behind Alefba:

  • سلامsalâm: hello
  • مامانmâmân: mom
  • باباbâbâ: dad
  • آبâb: water
  • مرسیmersi: thanks
  • بفرماbefarmâ: here you go

Phrases from taarof, the Persian way of being polite, are a good early set: children hear them at every family gathering. So are the ways to say thank you.

How much time should we spend each day?

A short daily habit is easier to keep than a long weekly lesson: for example, ten minutes of reading or a game before dinner, and Persian at the table.

Should we speak Persian or English at home?

Whatever your family can keep up. Some families give each parent one language; others keep Persian for certain times, such as meals, car rides or calls with grandparents. A rule you actually keep matters more than which rule you pick.

My child understands Persian but answers in English. What can I do?

This is very common. Keep speaking Persian, and give them the words they need: if they ask for water in English, answer آب می‌خوای؟ بگو آب (âb mikhâi? begu âb: do you want water? say âb). Make some things happen only in Persian, such as a bedtime story or a game, and keep it light rather than a punishment.

Regular video calls with grandparents and cousins who don't speak much English give children a real reason to try.

Should my child learn to read Persian, or just speak it?

Both, if you can. Speaking connects them to family; reading opens up books, signs, messages from relatives and poetry. Children who already read English can learn the Persian letters a few at a time, and Finglish (Persian in English letters, such as salâm) is a handy bridge until then.

What's the difference between spoken and book Persian?

Persian is written one way and spoken another. Books say khâne (house) and nân (bread), but people say khune and nun. Teach the spoken words for home, and show the book form when they start reading, so neither one comes as a surprise.

What age should children start learning Persian?

Any age. Babies and toddlers learn from hearing Persian, songs and picture books. Little ones can start on pictures, flashcards and tracing, and reading usually comes once a child can read in English.

What tools can help?

A mix works best: something to practice with every day, something to read, and people to talk to.

  • Alefba: letters with tracing, everyday words with recordings, short stories, games and practice conversations. The alphabet and the first 3 levels are free. A plan opens all 16 levels. There's a little mode for ages 3 to 6.
  • Tracing sheets: Alefba's printable tracing sheets for every letter are free.
  • Bilingual books: picture books in Persian, English and Finglish, such as the Englisi Farsi series. Its creator, Mona Kiani, talks about raising half-Iranian kids in this interview on Chai and Conversation.
  • A weekend Persian school, if there's one near you: many communities run classes on Saturdays or Sundays.
  • Celebrations: Nowruz and Shab-e Yalda come with their own words, food and stories. Alefba's Yalda pack is free.
  • Family: calls with grandparents, cousins and family friends.

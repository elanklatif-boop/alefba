What is taarof?

The Persian way of being polite, explained: when it happens, what to say, and how to tell when an offer is real.

Taarof (تعارف, ta'ârof) is the Persian etiquette of offering, refusing and insisting. A host offers again and again, a guest says no before saying yes, and everyone insists the other person goes first. It's a way of showing respect and warmth, and both sides usually know the steps.

In short: when someone offers, say no politely once or twice. If they keep insisting, accept and thank them.

What does taarof mean?

The word comes from Arabic and literally means “becoming acquainted.” In Persian it covers the everyday ways people show respect: offering tea and food, refusing at first, praising each other, holding the door, and fighting over the bill.

People also use it for an offer that isn't really meant: “he was just doing taarof.” That's why hosts say taârof nakon (don't do taarof) so often: it means “I really mean it, please have some.”

When does taarof happen?

Mostly around food, doors, money and gifts: whenever one person can offer something to another.

  • Tea and food. The host offers more, the guest says sir shodam (I'm full), the host says taârof nakon and offers again.
  • Doorways. Two people stop at a door and each says befarmâ or aval shomâ (you first) until one gives in.
  • The bill. Friends and relatives compete to pay: hesâb bâ man (the bill's on me).
  • Shops and taxis. A shopkeeper or driver may say ghâbel nadâre (it's nothing) when you ask what you owe. It's politeness: you still pay. Insist, often a few times, and they'll tell you the price.
  • Invitations. When invited to stay for dinner, guests often say mozâhem nemisham (I don't want to impose) before saying yes.
  • Gifts and compliments. The giver says ghâbel nadâre; the person thanked says sharmande (you're too kind) or ghorbunet beram.

How many times should you refuse in taarof?

There's no fixed number. Many people say no once or twice and accept when the host insists again, and you'll often hear “about three times” as a rule of thumb. With close family, people often skip it.

If you really don't want something, keep saying no warmly, for example dastet dard nakone, sir shodam (thank you, I'm full).

How can you tell if an offer is real?

Watch whether they insist. An offer made once and dropped may just be politeness; an offer repeated again and again is usually real.

With money it works the other way round: if a shopkeeper or driver says ghâbel nadâre, offer to pay again. They expect you to.

Is taarof fake or rude?

Not really. It's closer to a shared script: both people know the steps, and following them shows respect. Skipping it completely can come across as cold, while getting it a little wrong is easily forgiven.

Iranians joke about taarof a lot too, which is part of the fun of learning it.
